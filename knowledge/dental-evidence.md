# Dental evidence: a painful back tooth that may be cracked, for people on long-term steroids or other immune-lowering medicines

This is general background for an AI health companion. It is not a diagnosis and it does not replace the patient's dentist, endodontist or doctors. It is written generally, for an adult on long-term steroids (and sometimes other immune-lowering medicines) whose own steroid-making system (the HPA axis) may be suppressed, who may grind or clench their teeth (bruxism), and who has a back tooth that hurts on biting. It contains no information about any individual; their record lives in their private Sheet.

Grades: A = guideline, regulator, RCT or meta-analysis. B = observational study or review. C = case report or mechanism. D = community or directory. Full list under "References".

## 1. What a cracked tooth is, and why it hurts

**The five types.** The American Association of Endodontists (AAE) sorts long, lengthwise cracks into five types [1][2]:

| Type | What it is | Usual outlook |
|---|---|---|
| Craze line | Tiny lines in the outer enamel only. Almost every adult has some. | No pain. Cosmetic only. |
| Fractured cusp | One pointed corner of the chewing surface (a cusp) breaks, often next to a big filling. | Usually fixed with a new filling, onlay or crown. Rarely reaches the nerve. |
| Cracked tooth | A crack runs from the chewing surface down toward the root. The pieces have not separated (an incomplete or "greenstick" fracture). | Often savable. Outlook is less certain because cracks can grow. |
| Split tooth | The crack has gone all the way through. The tooth is in two pieces that can move apart. | Cannot be saved whole. Sometimes part can be kept, often it must come out. |
| Vertical root fracture | A crack that starts in the root. Almost always in a tooth that has had a root canal. | Usually means extraction or removing that root. |

The AAE stresses that finding a crack is not a diagnosis by itself. What matters is the health of the nerve (pulp) and the tissue around the root, so other tests are always needed [1].

**Why it hurts the way it does.** When you bite, the two sides of a crack flex apart a tiny amount. This tugs on fluid in the tooth and irritates the nerve. That is why the classic signs of a cracked tooth are sharp pain on chewing, sometimes worse when you let go (pain on release), and short, sharp pain with cold [1][3]. Over time, if germs travel down the crack, the nerve can become badly inflamed or die. Then the pain can come on by itself, and the gum or bone near the root can become infected [1][2]. Grinding and clenching put extra force on teeth and are a known cause of cracks [3][4].

**Why X-rays and CBCT often miss cracks.** A crack is often thinner than what an X-ray or CBCT scan can show. A regular X-ray is a flat (2D) picture of a 3D tooth, and a crack that runs side to side across the beam is invisible [1]. One review explains that CBCT detail is about 80 micrometres, so it "is not suitable for the clinical diagnosis of cracked teeth" or early root fractures [4]. Even for root fractures in root-filled teeth, a meta-analysis found CBCT caught about 78 of every 100 real fractures and was wrong on about 20 of every 100 healthy teeth. The authors concluded CBCT "is still not a good tool" compared with looking directly at the tooth [9]. So "no visible crack on the scan" does not mean "no crack". A thin line on CBCT also does not prove a crack, because scans can show false lines (artefacts).

## 2. How a specialist finds and judges a crack

No single test is enough. The AAE lists a set of steps used together [1]. A 2007 study of cracked teeth in a specialist clinic used most of them on every patient [8].

- **History and questions.** Which side hurts, what sets it off (biting, release, cold, heat), how long pain lasts, grinding habits, past fillings or root canals [1].
- **Bite test (Tooth Slooth or a similar bite stick).** The patient bites on a small plastic tool placed on one cusp at a time. Sharp pain on one cusp, especially on release, points to a crack there [3][4]. Some experts warn that hard pressure could make a crack grow, so it is done gently [4].
- **Transillumination (shining a bright light through the tooth).** Light passes through a whole tooth but stops at a real crack. The part beyond the crack looks darker. A craze line does not block the light [1][4].
- **Magnification or a dental operating microscope.** Lets the dentist see fine lines. One review notes 16 times magnification as best for enamel cracks [4].
- **Staining (a dye such as methylene blue).** The dye soaks into a crack and makes it easier to see. Old fillings may need to come out first [4].
- **Cold test and other pulp tests.** These check whether the nerve is healthy, mildly inflamed (reversible pulpitis) or badly inflamed or dead (irreversible pulpitis or necrosis). This result, not the crack itself, decides whether a root canal is needed [1].
- **Tapping (percussion).** Tenderness to tapping or biting can mean the tissue around the root is inflamed [1][4].
- **Periodontal probing (gently measuring the gum pocket).** A single deep, narrow pocket next to a crack is a warning sign that the crack runs deep down the root. This lowers the chance of saving the tooth [1].
- **A fresh periapical X-ray (IOPA).** A new, close-up X-ray of the tooth and root tip. It is the standard way to check for decay between teeth and for changes at the root tip. It is quick, cheap and low-dose, and it is what a CBCT report means when it says "rule out on IOPA" [1].
- **Looking inside during treatment.** Sometimes the crack is only confirmed when an old filling is removed, or during root canal access [1].

Some molars have unusual canal shapes (for example a C-shaped canal) that are harder to clean and fill. That is one more reason to have any root canal on such a tooth done by an endodontist with a microscope.

## 3. Treatment options, in usual order

The plan depends on where the crack is, how deep it goes, and the state of the nerve [1][2].

**Step 1: Protect the tooth with cusp coverage.** A crack does not heal like a broken bone [2]. The aim is to hold the tooth together so it stops flexing.

- **Short term:** an orthodontic band (a thin steel ring), a temporary crown, or a bonded composite splint. If pain settles with a band or temporary crown, that is a good sign [1][4].
- **Long term:** a full crown or an onlay that covers the cusps (cuspal coverage). A full crown gives the most protection but "does not guarantee success in all cases" [2]. Reviews favour full or partial coverage over plain fillings for cracked teeth [4].
- **Occlusal adjustment:** gently reshaping the biting surface so the cracked tooth takes less force while it is being treated [4].

**Step 2: Root canal treatment if the nerve is involved.** If tests show the nerve is badly inflamed or dead, a root canal is done, then a crown [1][2]. Without that diagnosis, a root canal is not done just because a crack exists [1].

**Step 3: Extraction if the tooth is split or the root is fractured.** A split tooth with a crack deep down the root, or a vertical root fracture, usually cannot be saved. Then extraction, followed by an implant or bridge, is the predictable choice [1][2].

**What the evidence says about outlook.**

- **The US National Dental Practice-Based Research Network cracked tooth registry** followed 2,858 patients, each with a cracked back tooth, seen by 209 dentists for 3 years [5].
  - Only 37 teeth (1.3%) were extracted. More than 98% survived [5].
  - 67 of 478 teeth that were restored (14%) had a treatment failure. Failure was more likely with fillings inside the tooth (intracoronal) than with full or partial coverage [5].
  - About 80% of teeth that dentists chose to watch were still just being watched at 3 years [5].
  - Only 3% of cracked teeth went on to break. Breaking was more likely in upper teeth, teeth worn through the enamel (a sign of grinding), and cracks a probe could catch [6].
  - In the first year, untreated cracked teeth were mostly stable. Symptoms more often got better than worse [7].
  - Important limit: most of these teeth had a visible crack but mild or no symptoms. A tooth with sharp bite pain plus hot and cold pain is in a more serious group.
- **Painful cracked teeth with a mildly inflamed nerve:** in a 6-year study, 127 patients had a crown placed without a root canal. About 20% (27) needed a root canal, all within about 6 months. None of the other 100 needed one later [8].
- **Cracked teeth that needed a root canal:** a small study cited by the AAE found about 85.5% survival at 2 years. Outlook was worse with several cracks, with the last tooth in the row, and with a deep gum pocket before treatment [1].
- The AAE advises telling patients honestly that the long-term outlook for a cracked tooth is "questionable", because cracks can grow, but that many teeth are saved [1].

**Night guard and bruxism.** For someone who grinds and clenches, protecting the remaining teeth matters, especially after earlier cracked teeth.

- A hard acrylic night guard (occlusal splint) spreads biting force. One review says splints can help prevent cracked teeth, but only if worn regularly [4].
- A Cochrane review found no clear evidence that splints stop the grinding itself during sleep. It said there may be some benefit for protecting teeth from wear [10]. So a guard is mainly protection, not a cure.
- Implants do not have the "shock absorber" of a natural tooth. If there are implants, the dentist may want the guard to cover them too. This is a question for the dentist.

## 4. Who to see, and how not to wait months

**The right specialist is an endodontist.** Endodontists specialise in diagnosing cracked teeth and saving them [2]. In India, the matching qualification is an **MDS in Conservative Dentistry and Endodontics** (a 3-year specialist degree after the basic BDS).

**Useful bodies in India:**

- **Indian Endodontic Society (IES):** India's largest society only for endodontics, with over 4,000 life members and chapters in several cities [11].
- **Indian Board of Endodontics:** set up by the IES. It offers board certification for endodontists [12].
- **Indian Dental Association (IDA):** over 75,000 members in more than 450 local branches. A local branch can help point to specialists nearby [13].

**Getting a timely second opinion locally:**

1. Ask the general dentist for the names of two or three other MDS endodontists. One specialist being booked up does not mean all are.
2. Ask the local IDA branch, or the IES, for endodontists in your city [11][13].
3. Try the Department of Conservative Dentistry and Endodontics at a postgraduate dental college hospital in the city. These departments run specialist clinics, often with microscopes, and can sometimes see patients sooner.
4. When booking, say clearly: "sharp pain on biting even soft food, hot and cold pain, tender gum, on long-term steroids and immunosuppressants". This helps clinics see that it is not a routine visit.
5. If pain is getting worse, or any red flag in section 7 appears, do not wait for any appointment.

**What to bring:**

- The CBCT files themselves (the DICOM data on a disc or USB), not only the printed report. The specialist may want to scroll through the slices.
- Any older X-rays, plus records of earlier problem teeth: cracks, root canals, extractions and implants.
- A written timeline of symptoms: what triggers the pain, how long it lasts, whether it wakes you at night.
- A full medicine list with doses, including steroids, immune-lowering medicines and painkillers.
- A short medical summary, including any adrenal (cortisol) test results.
- Contact details for the doctor who manages the steroids, so the dentist can check with them.
- Any steroid alert card.

## 5. Dental treatment on long-term steroids and immunosuppressants

**Healing and infection risk.**

- Steroids and other immune-lowering medicines lower the body's defences. The NHS lists "getting more infections" as a possible serious side effect of steroids. The risk is higher with long-term use [30].
- A review of surgical studies found that people on steroids for 30 days or more before surgery may have 2 to 5 times more wound problems. The risk varies with dose, other illnesses and the type of surgery [17].
- UK dental guidance (SDCEP) says people with a weak immune system (immunocompromised) are at higher risk of complications from infection, including sepsis. Dentists should have a lower threshold for giving antibiotics if there is an infection [16].
- This is a reason to treat a problem tooth before it becomes infected. It is not, by itself, a reason to refuse treatment.

**Are antibiotics needed "just in case" before dental work?** Honest answer: there is no clear rule for people on immunosuppressants.

- The American Dental Association recommends routine preventive antibiotics only for certain high-risk heart conditions. In general it does not recommend them for people with joint replacements [14]. It leaves other cases to the treating doctor.
- A Cochrane review of antibiotics before tooth extraction found low-certainty evidence that they reduce infection after wisdom tooth removal in healthy people. **None** of the studies looked at people with weak immune systems. The review says such patients "need an individualised approach in consultation with their treating medical specialist" [15].
- So the dentist and the doctor who prescribes the immune-lowering medicines should decide together, case by case.

**Steroid "stress dose" or "sick day" cover for dental work.** When the body's own steroid system is suppressed, it may not make the extra cortisol needed during illness, surgery or other stress. Missing this can lead to an adrenal crisis, which is a medical emergency [18][22].

What the main sources say:

- **Who is at risk (UK):** people taking prednisolone 5 mg a day or more for over 4 weeks should get an NHS Steroid Emergency Card. They should have steroid cover for surgery and invasive procedures [22]. The card is meant to be shown to staff before a procedure [23].
- **ESE / Endocrine Society joint guideline (2024):** "Minor surgery including any procedure requiring local anaesthesia" counts as needing stress cover [18].
  - For people on prednisone under 10 mg a day, it suggests raising the dose to 10 mg, given 1 hour before the procedure.
  - For people already on prednisone 10 mg or more, no increase is usually needed for minor stress.
  - Procedures under general anaesthesia need injected hydrocortisone at the start [18]. UK anaesthetic guidance says the same [20].
- **NICE (2024):** people on prednisolone 10 mg a day or more usually do not need extra sick-day doses [19].
- **Society for Endocrinology patient sheet ("Steroid Sick Day Rules"):**
  - Minor dental surgery: take 5 mg prednisolone or 20 mg hydrocortisone 1 hour before, then a double dose for 24 hours, then back to normal.
  - Major dental surgery: an injection of 100 mg hydrocortisone may be needed. Discuss this with the dentist in advance [21].
- **UK pharmacy advice for primary adrenal insufficiency** (a different, permanent condition): an extra oral dose 1 hour before minor dental work, and cover for 24 hours after oral surgery. It advises sending extractions to hospital-based care [24]. It is not written for steroid-induced suppression, but it shows the cautious UK approach.
- **The other side of the evidence:**
  - A 2004 critical review in the British Dental Journal found few real cases of adrenal crisis from dentistry. It concluded that routine dentistry under local anaesthetic does not need extra steroid [25].
  - A 2023 randomised, triple-blind trial of 270 patients in India (Journal of the American Dental Association) found that people on long-term steroids for reasons other than primary adrenal insufficiency had minor oral surgery under local anaesthetic safely on their usual daily dose. This held when their medical conditions were well controlled [26].

**Bottom line for the companion:** the guidelines and the trial do not fully agree. The choice depends on the exact dose, test results and overall health. **The dose decision belongs to the doctors who manage the steroids, not to the dentist alone and not to the companion.** The companion should help the patient ask for a written plan before any procedure, especially an extraction. The plan should say what to take, when, and what to do if they feel faint, sick or very unwell afterwards. Steroids should never be stopped or cut without the prescriber.

## 6. Self-care while waiting

These steps can reduce pain and lower the chance of the crack growing. They do not fix it.

- **Chew on the other side.** Avoid biting on the sore tooth at all if possible.
- **Avoid hard, chewy and sticky foods:** nuts, hard sweets, ice, bones, popcorn kernels, chikki, toffee, hard crusts. Cut food small.
- **Avoid very hot or very cold food and drink.** Let tea and coffee cool a little. Avoid ice-cold water on that side.
- **Do not chew ice, pens or nails.** Be aware of clenching during the day. Try lips together, teeth apart.
- **Night guard:** wear it every night if one has been made. If not, ask the dentist whether a guard or a temporary band or crown can be fitted now, before the specialist visit [1][4].
- **Keep the area clean:** gentle brushing and cleaning between the teeth, even when sore. This lowers infection risk.
- **Pain relief, only as the patient's own doctors allow:**
  - Paracetamol is often the first choice. With liver disease, the safe dose must be set by the liver doctor.
  - The NHS says ibuprofen may not be suitable for people with a liver condition or a stomach ulcer, and that it may not mix well with steroid medicines [28]. Its prednisolone page also lists NSAIDs such as ibuprofen and aspirin among medicines that may not mix well [27].
  - So **check with a doctor or pharmacist before taking any NSAID** (ibuprofen, diclofenac, naproxen, aspirin, or combination "cold and flu" or "pain" tablets).
  - Do not take more than the recommended dose of any painkiller [29].
- **Do not put aspirin or clove oil directly on the gum.** These can burn the gum.
- **Keep the specialist appointment, and keep looking for an earlier one** (section 4).

## 7. Red flags: get same-day or emergency care

A cracked tooth can let germs reach the nerve and root, causing an abscess [1][2]. People on steroids and immunosuppressants are at higher risk of complications from infection, including sepsis [16]. So **act early, and do not wait to see if it settles**.

**Call an ambulance (in India, 112 or 108) or go to a hospital emergency department now if there is:**

- Swelling of the face, jaw or neck that is growing [29].
- Trouble breathing, speaking or swallowing [29].
- Swelling near or around the eye, a painful eye, or a change in eyesight [29].
- Being unable to open the mouth properly [29].
- A lot of swelling inside the mouth or under the tongue [29].
- Feeling very unwell: high fever, shivering (rigors), confusion, fast heartbeat, or feeling faint or dizzy. These can be signs of sepsis [16].
- Severe weakness, vomiting, low blood pressure or collapse. In someone with a suppressed steroid system, this may be an **adrenal crisis**. Follow the steroid emergency plan and tell staff about the long-term steroids [21][23].

**Same-day dentist or doctor (not next week) if there is:**

- New swelling of the gum or face, even if small [16].
- A pimple or boil on the gum, or a bad taste or discharge (pus).
- Redness that is spreading, or tender glands under the jaw [16].
- Fever or feeling generally unwell alongside the tooth pain [16].
- Pain that now comes on by itself, keeps you awake, or is no longer helped by allowed painkillers [1].

Tell every clinician about the steroids, other immune-lowering medicines and any liver condition. When unwell, a steroid dose change may be needed, which the doctors decide [18][19].

## 8. Questions to ask the endodontist

1. From your tests today, is this a cracked tooth, decay, or both? Which type of crack, and how deep do you think it goes?
2. Is the nerve healthy, mildly inflamed, or badly inflamed or dead? Do I need a root canal?
3. Did probing find a deep, narrow pocket? What does that mean for saving the tooth?
4. Can you protect the tooth today with a band, temporary crown or onlay while we decide?
5. If a root canal is needed, is the canal shape unusual, and will you use a microscope?
6. What are the realistic chances of keeping this tooth for 5 years or more, compared with extraction and an implant?
7. Will you talk to the doctor who manages my steroids about steroid cover and antibiotics before any procedure? Can I have that plan in writing?
8. What night guard do you recommend for my grinding, and should it cover any implants?
